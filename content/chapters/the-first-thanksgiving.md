---

In 1621 the English at Plymouth feasted for three days with Ousamequin and about ninety men. Only one eyewitness wrote it down.

Ousamequin made an agreement with the newcomers after epidemics devastated Wampanoag communities. For men, women and children captured in later wars, English victory could mean slavery. Centuries later, UAINE recounts, Wamsutta James refused to deliver the organizers’ replacement speech at an anniversary banquet; Deb Haaland opened an investigation into the schools her own family had attended. The meal, the holiday and the mourning each have a history.
