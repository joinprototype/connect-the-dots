---

Between 1946 and 1958 the United States fired 67 nuclear weapons in the Marshall Islands. Between 1949 and 1989 the Soviet Union fired 456 at Semipalatinsk, in Kazakhstan. People lived beside both sites.

The short answer, from the records read here: on 1 March 1954 the biggest U.S. test, Bravo, put fallout on Marshallese atolls, 28 U.S. servicemen and 23 Japanese fishermen. The Atomic Energy Commission’s chairman counted 236 Marshallese moved; the 401 people of Ailuk were not moved; the National Cancer Institute counts 14,000 residents exposed to the whole testing program. A Marshall Islands tribunal set up under the 1986 agreement awarded over $87 million for personal injuries and had paid $71.7 million by the end of 2004. It awarded about $2.3 billion for the lands of four atolls, by these sources, almost all of it unpaid; Enewetak’s counsel puts the unpaid total at over $3 billion in today’s money. In Kazakhstan the Soviet military ran the site in secrecy, and the Kazakh republic’s president closed it by decree on 29 August 1991. Counts of the people exposed there run from 220,000 to more than 1.5 million, depending on who counted.

Each government gets its own say, in its own documents. What the record does not show is marked where it comes up. Not read: the Soviet Ministry of Defence’s and the Soviet government’s own statements of 1989 and 1991 (the Wilson Center archive refused a download); the Dispensary No. 4’s own records; the basis of Kazakhstan’s 1.3 million figure and the amounts paid under its 1992 law; the Marshall Islands government’s full reply to the 2004 U.S. report; the primary transcript of Eisenbud’s remark of January 1956 (the Marshall Islands Journal places it on Utrik, with a 15-roentgen exposure that fits Utirik; Enewetak’s counsel attaches it to Rongelap); the Bikinians’ own accounts beyond the rough scan of one journal paper; Japanese-language records of the Lucky Dragon; and the day of Suleimenov’s television appeal, which sources date the 26th or the 28th of February. Death tolls and doses differ by who counted, so the story says who. For the bombs themselves see [[the-most-terrible-bomb]].
