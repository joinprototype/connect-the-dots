summary: On the night of 2 to 3 December 1984, gas escaped from a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India. Here are the people of Bhopal in their own words, the owners, the counts, the water, and what the courts decided from 1989 to 2023.
---

On the night of 2 to 3 December 1984, gas from a pesticide plant in Bhopal, India, spread over the homes around it. Counts of the dead depend on who counts and when: India told Parliament 1,430 four months on; the Supreme Court's 2011 order records 5,295; the Madhya Pradesh relief department reported 15,248 by October 2003; Amnesty International puts the first three days alone at 7,000 to 10,000. Union Carbide Corporation, of Danbury, Connecticut, held 50.9 percent of the Indian company that ran the plant, and a U.S. appeals court wrote that 22 percent was "owned or controlled by the government of India." In 1989 India's Supreme Court ordered a $470 million settlement. In 2010 a Bhopal court convicted seven people and that company of causing death by negligence, the two-year maximum after a 1996 ruling cut the charge. Union Carbide Corporation and its 1984 chairman, Warren Anderson, did not appear in the Indian criminal case, and the court declared both absconders; Anderson had been bailed in 1984 on a promise to return and went home to the United States. The site and its water are still disputed.

Where counts differ, the story says who counted. Union Carbide, Dow and the others named get their reply in their own words, and the story does not decide what caused the leak. Limits of what was read: the 1991 review order only as the Supreme Court quoted it in 2023; the 2023 order in the copy LiveLaw published, because the court's own copy could not be fetched; the 1989 order in a copy marked as not the official version; the Second Circuit's 1987 opinion and the Attorney General's 2001 opinion in copies Union Carbide posted; and the 2010 trial judgment not at all, only the Supreme Court's and the press's accounts of it.
