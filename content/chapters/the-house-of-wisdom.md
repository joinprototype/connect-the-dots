---

Baghdad was founded in 762. In one popular telling its House of Wisdom was a great academy where Arabic scholars translated the Greeks, invented algebra and taught Europe, until the Mongols drowned the books in the Tigris in 1258. In the opposite telling, Europe owed Baghdad little or nothing.

This story sets both beside the scholars who work from the sources, and gives each scholar their own say. On the House of Wisdom, Dimitri Gutas finds a library, most likely a bureau, while MacTutor's biographies still describe an academy where Greek works were translated. The sources read also show a large movement of translation into Arabic, a Latin Europe that read Arabic books from the twelfth century in Spain, the Mongol sack of 1258, and a dispute over why Islamic science slowed afterwards. The ink in the Tigris is a tale.

Most quotes are from translations, scholars and reference works. Two outlets are marked: a supporter's review of Gouguenheim's book, and a bookseller's site that prints Gutas's pages. Not read: Gutas's book itself, Saliba's 2007 book (a review only), Santoyo's article and Biran's paper (abstracts only), Burnett's 2001 study of Toledo, Gouguenheim's book and his replies. The story says so where it matters.
