---

Baghdad was founded in 762. MacTutor describes its House of Wisdom as an academy translating Greek philosophy and science. Dimitri Gutas disputes that institutional picture. Latin readers did study Arabic books; writers disagree about Europe's debt to them. The Mongols sacked Baghdad in 1258, but the ink-black Tigris is a tale.

This story sets both beside the scholars who work from the sources, and gives each scholar their own say. On the House of Wisdom, Dimitri Gutas finds a library, most likely a bureau, while MacTutor's biographies still describe an academy where Greek works were translated. The sources read also show a large movement of translation into Arabic, a Latin Europe that read Arabic books from the twelfth century in Spain, the Mongol sack of 1258, and a dispute over why Islamic science slowed afterwards. The ink in the Tigris is a tale.

The reasons here come from public prefaces, later recollections and historians' reconstructions. They do not establish a contrast between public declarations and verified private intentions. Most quotes are from translations, scholars and reference works. Two outlets are marked: a supporter's review of Gouguenheim's book, and a bookseller's site that prints Gutas's pages. Not read: Gutas's book itself, Saliba's 2007 book (a review only), Santoyo's article and Biran's paper (abstracts only), Burnett's 2001 study of Toledo, Gouguenheim's book and his replies. The story says so where it matters.
