---

In 1600 a group of London merchants received a royal charter. By 1858 their company had collected the taxes of Bengal, sold opium that came to be fought over in China, been examined by Parliament, and lost its government of India to the Crown.

Nearly every line here comes from Parliament's own records, the Company's letters, treaties, and the books of historians. Most of the historians quoted are British writers of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and the story says who is speaking. The Company's own record reaches this story through a printing by Hunter, and the story says so. Voices from India are here too: the petitions of Indian officers in the famine as Hunter cites them, Sayyid Ahmad Khan on 1857, Lin's letter to the Queen, and Dadabhai Naoroji by way of those who write about him. Death tolls and sums taken from India are estimates, and where they differ the story says who counted and how. The Company, the Crown and Parliament were different actors at different dates, and the story names which one it means. The Company, its officers, those who accused them, and those who suffered under them each get their say, and the story does not pick one.
