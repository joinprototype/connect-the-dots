Did Alexander “unite East and West”? Waldemar Heckel traces the idea of a civilising conquest to Plutarch and its later development under the British Empire; he rejects its method while recognizing an exchange of ideas. Alexander’s conquests did bring territories under one command, and at Susa he joined royal families through marriage. They also destroyed cities: Plutarch reports over 6,000 killed and over 30,000 enslaved at Thebes alone. After Alexander died in 323 BCE, his commanders divided the provinces and fought over them. Following his road shows what that unity cost and what survived it. [Heckel, pp. 3–4](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/42471/excerpt/9780521842471_excerpt.pdf); [Plutarch, 11–12](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Alexander*/3.html).

The fullest surviving narratives were written centuries later. Diodorus wrote in the first century BCE, roughly 300 years after Alexander; Waldemar Heckel identifies his principal source for Alexander’s reign as Cleitarchus’s lost history. Curtius, on the usual but uncertain first-century CE dating, wrote roughly 350–400 years later, drawing chiefly on Cleitarchus and adding Ptolemy. Their agreement can reflect a shared predecessor. For the succession in Books 18–20, Oldfather identifies a different principal source: Hieronymus of Cardia’s lost history, informed by service to Eumenes and then Antigonus.

Plutarch wrote in the late first or early second century CE, roughly 400–450 years later. His biography cites lost works by Aristobulus, Chares and Onesicritus, as well as letters attributed to Alexander. Arrian wrote in the second century CE, roughly 450 years after Alexander, chiefly using the lost accounts of Ptolemy and Aristobulus, both expedition participants. Arrian trusted them; Heckel stresses that both defended the king and omitted damaging episodes. Their words sit here beside a contemporary Babylonian diary and the older royal inscriptions at Persepolis and Behistun. Ancient army, casualty and treasury figures remain their writers’ figures.
