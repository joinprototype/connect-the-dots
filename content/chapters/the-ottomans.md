title: Six Hundred Years
subtitle: How a frontier chief's people built an empire across three continents, how it ran, and what ended it in 1922
summary: "From about 1300 to 1922 one dynasty ruled in Anatolia, then in Europe and the Arab lands. Here is the road in the words of Ibn Battuta, Doukas, Ibn Iyas, Busbecq, the sultan's edict of 1839, Morgenthau and Mustafa Kemal, with the historian Donald Quataert on whether it was decline, and each explanation of the end named for whoever gives it."
---

Around 1300 a Turcoman frontier chief named Osman led some 40,000 tents of nomads, and rivals led more. On 1 November 1922 an assembly at Ankara ended the sultanate his dynasty held. Between lie the capture of Constantinople, the conquest of Egypt, two sieges of Vienna, a century of loans and reform, and the First World War.

Here is the road in the words of those who saw it: Ibn Battuta, the Byzantine historian Doukas (read in English), the Cairo chronicler Ibn Iyas, the Habsburg ambassador Busbecq, the sultan's edict of 1839, the 1876 constitution, treaties, the U.S. ambassador Morgenthau and Mustafa Kemal; and the historian Donald Quataert, whose survey is the main modern voice. Doukas, Busbecq, Morgenthau and Kemal each wrote on one side of something, and the story says so. It read no Ottoman chronicle, no Serbian account of Kosovo and no other medieval narrative of 1453 than Doukas's, with Phrantzes's census read through Edwin Pears's retelling; it did not read the historians Suraiya Faroqhi or Şükrü Hanioğlu, so the debate over "decline" is told through one historian. It repeats no tale about Osman that it could not source. The deportations and killings of 1915 are told in the story "1915"; here they are one scene. Dates before 1582 are given as the historians give them, in the Julian calendar.
