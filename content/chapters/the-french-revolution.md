By 1786 the monarchy’s deficit was one-sixth of its budget, according to **Liberty, Equality, Fraternity**. Louis XVI needed consent to raise money; the Estates-General became a National Assembly claiming to speak for France. His refusals, failed flight and the uprising of August 1792 broke the monarchy, and the Convention sent him to the guillotine. [Calonne’s proposal and fiscal background](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/258), [the Estates-General](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/1087), [the fall of the king’s powers](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/319).

The violence that followed was counted later, and the counts differ. **Arno J. Mayer, citing Donald Greer, estimates 35,000–40,000 deaths under the official Terror**: tribunal sentences, executions without trial and prison deaths, March 1793–July/August 1794. For the **Vendée civil war**, **Peter McPhee** reports recent estimates of **up to 170,000 insurgents and 30,000 republican troops**. Those war losses overlap with the Terror’s victims; the totals cannot be added. [Mayer, p. 363 and note 102](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691090153/the-furies), [McPhee’s summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-genocide/vicious-civil-war-in-the-french-revolution/576E50A9A665BB2501A8BFE71302302C).

**Lives behind the counts.** Mayer’s **35,000–40,000** includes roughly **17,000 death sentences** by special tribunals and commissions, **10,000–12,000 executions after capture without trial**, and thousands of deaths in prison. He cites Donald Greer’s 1935 statistical study. Madame Roland and Marie-Antoinette were among those executed; at Lucs-sur-Boulogne, Mayer describes women and children killed alongside other inhabitants. His categories reach far beyond the Paris guillotine. [Mayer, pp. 354–355, 363–364; p. 370, note 102](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691090153/the-furies).

For the **Vendée**, Mayer surveys **150,000–500,000 battle and civilian deaths**, including the accompanying and subsequent terror. McPhee calls **500,000 rebel deaths** exaggerated and reports more accepted recent estimates of **up to 170,000 insurgents and 30,000 republican troops**. His published summary does not give their calculations. **François Lebrun’s** 1986 review instead estimates a **100,000–170,000 population shortfall** over the revolutionary decade, comparing average births with censuses and extrapolating from the Vendée department and Beaupréau district. He warns that missing inhabitants cannot all be counted as people directly killed by war or republican terror. These measures overlap and cannot be added. [Mayer, p. 309](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691090153/the-furies), [McPhee](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-genocide/vicious-civil-war-in-the-french-revolution/576E50A9A665BB2501A8BFE71302302C), [Lebrun, p. 357](https://www.persee.fr/doc/abpo_0399-0826_1986_num_93_3_3228).

**Reynald Sécher** called the repression genocide; Lebrun challenged his demographic arithmetic and geographical coverage. McPhee rejects genocide as the explanation, arguing that the victims were not killed as a distinctive Vendéan people or for being devout Catholics; he calls it a civil war with atrocities. Their disagreement leaves the burnings and killings in view. [Lebrun’s review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/abpo_0399-0826_1986_num_93_3_3228), [McPhee](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-world-history-of-genocide/vicious-civil-war-in-the-french-revolution/576E50A9A665BB2501A8BFE71302302C).

**The king’s four votes.** The modern **French National Assembly** account gives **642 of 718 present** for guilt, **423 of 721** against appeal to the people, **361 of 721** for unconditional death plus **26** for death with a possible delay, and **380** against reprieve; it gives no total for that last ballot. Its majority of one vote is the narrowest absolute majority for unconditional death. It does **not** describe a simple one-vote contest between death and all its opponents: its combined death count is **387**. Liberty, Equality, Fraternity calls the guilt vote unanimous and repeats the one-vote penalty shorthand; the accounts differ. [National Assembly’s historical account](https://www.assemblee-nationale.fr/dyn/histoire-et-patrimoine/revolution-francaise/proces-et-execution-de-louis-xvi), [Liberty, Equality, Fraternity](https://revolution.chnm.org/d/327).

**Two popular versions.** In the cake tale, Marie-Antoinette answers news that people have no bread by telling them to eat cake. **John M. Cunningham, writing for Britannica**, finds no historical evidence that she said it. He traces Rousseau’s version to an unnamed princess while Marie-Antoinette was a child, and notes that the earliest known printed link to her, in Alphonse Karr’s 1843 writing, denied the attribution. Cunningham identifies the attribution as a legend. [Britannica’s account](https://www.britannica.com/story/did-marie-antoinette-really-say-let-them-eat-cake).

The letter popularly circulated as Westermann’s **“There is no more Vendée”** is also a claim to test. Mayer reproduces a boast attributed to Westermann after Savenay about wiping out the Vendée, killing women and trampling children. His repetition alone does not establish the letter’s authenticity; it cannot stand as an authenticated confession. The burnings and the massacre at Lucs rest on the separate accounts described above. [Mayer, pp. 339–340, 352–355](https://press.princeton.edu/books/paperback/9780691090153/the-furies).

**Why the monarchy fell.** [[eugene-white|Eugene N. White]] argued in 1989 that severe peacetime deficits emerged when earlier successful policies were abandoned in the mid-1780s. His 1995 account emphasizes a persistent deficit and the struggle over who would bear adjustment. **François Velde and David Weir** emphasize institutional obstacles to raising taxes. The American war belongs in that financial history, but these readings differ over how borrowing and political choices brought down the crown. [White’s 1989 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jechis/v49y1989i03p545-568_00.html), [[white-1995-politics-of-government-finance|White’s 1995 account]], [[velde-weir-1992-financial-market-and-debt-policy|Velde and Weir’s account]], [The Bill for America](/c/the-bill-for-america/).

The Assembly itself blamed ignorance, neglect and contempt of rights for public calamities and governmental corruption. Lafayette proposed a declaration; the deputies wrote rights into their new political order. The **Cambridge coursebook** credits Enlightenment ideas with influence while warning that their weight is difficult to measure. [The Declaration](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/18th_century/rightsof.asp), [[enlightenment-ideas-in-france|the coursebook’s account]].

In [[georges-lefebvre|Georges Lefebvre]]’s **Marxist reading**, economic power, ability and confidence had passed largely to the bourgeoisie while nobility and clergy retained legal precedence. The Revolution brought law into line with that changed balance; his account followed distinct aristocratic, bourgeois, urban popular and peasant revolutions. [[francois-furet|François Furet]]’s **revisionism** challenged the necessity of a bourgeois revolution: capitalism and bourgeois dominance had appeared elsewhere without one. He gave political language and intellectuals a force that could not be read straight from economic conditions. **Mark Andrew Day** discusses and quotes both historians, using R. R. Palmer’s English Lefebvre and Elborg Forster’s English Furet. [Day, pp. 39–40, 51–54](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/6008/1/274988.pdf).

**Limits of the reading.** The Convention’s roll calls and Louis’s complete formal defence were not read for this story. Neither were the royal debt stock and debt-service accounts, Greer’s registers, the calculations behind McPhee’s modern estimates, Sécher’s full English argument, or Westermann’s original letter and studies of its authenticity. The debt total and the share of revenue spent servicing it are therefore not given. Marie-Antoinette’s farewell is in Yonge’s English text; her trial transcript was not read. Durand’s explanation of Thermidor is a later memoir; Turreau’s orders are in Mayer’s rendering; Lefebvre and Furet are read through Day’s excerpts.
