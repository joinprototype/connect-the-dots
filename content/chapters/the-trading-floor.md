Congress made the duty explicit in 2012, required trade reports within 45 days, then narrowed online disclosure in 2013. By 30 September 2026 its members could still own and trade individual stocks. Whether they used what they learned in office remained a question requiring evidence about information as well as transactions.

**Counting the returns: the studies, by publication date.** These papers ask whether disclosed investing earned unusual returns. Their populations, holding periods and comparisons differ; they do not count proven violations of the STOCK Act.

| Published | Who counted, and how | What they found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| December 2004 | Alan Ziobrowski, Ping Cheng, James Boyd and Brigitte Ziobrowski copied senators’ disclosed common-stock purchases and sales in 1993–1998 into portfolios, testing trade-size-weighted returns against market models. | Purchases beat the market by 85 basis points a month; sales lagged by a statistically insignificant 12. The paper found no abnormal returns in 1997–1998, no reliable party difference, and warned against inferring illegal activity. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. [Senate study](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v39y2004i04p661-676_00.html). |
| April 2011 | Ziobrowski, Boyd, Cheng and Ziobrowski applied the same transaction method to more than 16,000 transactions by about 300 House members in odd-numbered years from 1985 to 2001. | Copied purchases beat the market by 55 basis points a month, about 6% annually. [House study](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/buspol/v13y2011i01p1-22_00.html). |
| April 2013 | Andrew Eggers and Jens Hainmueller reinterpreted the earlier studies and reconstructed members’ portfolio holdings in 2004–2008, including stocks they kept rather than just those they traded. | They found no information advantage in either period and found the average member would have earned more in an index fund; their December 2011 working paper estimated annual underperformance of 2–3%. Four senators accounted for nearly half the trades in the early Senate sample, helping explain why copied trades could differ from the average member’s result. [Capitol Losses](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/46513). |
| 2014 | Hainmueller and Eggers examined members’ investments in local firms and campaign contributors in 2004–2008. | These connected holdings generally outperformed members’ other holdings; poor returns elsewhere explained overall underperformance. This compared parts of members’ portfolios, not every holding with the market. [Political Capital](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/57734). |
| 2017 | Joshua Hall, Serkan Karadas and Minh Tam Tammy Schlosky compared politicians’ transactions before and after election in 2004–2010, constructing portfolios and testing them with asset-pricing models. | Evidence of an edge before election was weak and inconsistent; abnormal returns emerged after two consecutive terms, only among members on powerful committees. The authors said their results did not imply wrongdoing. [Before and after election](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eme:jfeppp:jfep-12-2016-0095). |
| 2022 | William Belmont, Bruce Sacerdote, Ranjan Sehgal and Ian Van Hoek examined members’ public-equity trades from January 2012 through December 2020, testing subsequent returns, including six-month holding periods. | They found no superior performance overall or among senators accused of informed trading; even very high returns were consistent with random selection. They expressly could not rule out particular insider trades hidden by averages or poor subsequent returns. [Evidence from the STOCK Act](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v207y2022ics0047272722000044.html). |
| November 2025 | Shang-Jin Wei and Yifan Zhou’s NBER working paper followed 20 of 47 congressional leaders in 1995–2021 who traded both before and after promotion, comparing them with matched ordinary members. | After promotion, leaders outperformed their matches by up to 47 percentage points a year. The authors cautioned that these twenty leaders did not represent Congress as a whole. [Captain Gains](https://www.nber.org/papers/w34524). |
| April 2026 | Haotian Chen and Bruce Sacerdote’s NBER working paper covered all members and immediate families in 2012–2023, comparing portfolios with market benchmarks and trade timing with professional recommendations and retail-investor sentiment. Its party-leader comparison measured returns after trades against the market, rather than changes after promotion against matched members. | Average portfolios underperformed or at best matched benchmarks; their positions and timing followed public signals rather than consistently anticipating prices. Party leaders’ trades underperformed the CRSP value-weighted market benchmark by 4.37 percentage points at one year. This later sample and different comparison do not directly replicate Wei and Zhou’s promotion study. [Capital in the Capitol](https://www.nber.org/papers/w35041). |

The Act made the duty explicit and the transactions easier to inspect. The studies do not establish that it ended informed trading, or that any named member broke the law: that requires evidence of what the person knew and used.

In separate cases, [[senators-stock-sales-early-2020|Richard Burr]] denied trading on private information, the Justice Department ended its inquiry and the SEC closed its own with no action; the Ethics Committee unanimously dismissed [[ethics-ptr-referrals-2022|Tom Suozzi’s referral]]; and [[buyer-insider-trading-case|Stephen Buyer]], who maintained his innocence, was convicted after leaving Congress, had his conviction affirmed by the Second Circuit and his Supreme Court appeal rejected, and received a June 2026 pardon.
