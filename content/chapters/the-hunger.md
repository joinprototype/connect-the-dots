title: The Hunger
subtitle: The Soviet famine of 1930–33, from the steppe of Kazakhstan to Ukraine and the Volga, and the argument over what to call it
summary: Millions starved after Stalin's collectivisation; authors put the Union-wide toll between 5.5 and 14 million. Kazakhstan's famine is far less known, and Sarah Cameron puts the loss at more than a third of all Kazakhs. Parliaments, a Kyiv court and historians dispute whether it was planned and whether it was genocide; each side's position is given, though Davies and Wheatcroft's book could not be read.
---

In the early 1930s the Soviet state took the grain and the herds from farms across the Union. Famine followed in Kazakhstan, Ukraine, the Volga and the North Caucasus. How many died depends on who counts: totals in the sources run from 5.5 million to about 14 million.

Kazakhstan's famine is little known in the West, Sarah Cameron writes. Werth notes that historians have researched the famines in Ukraine and the Kuban more thoroughly than the others. Each number below is given with its author, and the method where the author gives one. Whether the famine was planned, and whether it was genocide, are questions that parliaments, a court and historians answer differently, and the story sets out each position.

Lines marked "according to" are one author's reading, not a record of fact. One book at the centre of the dispute, Davies and Wheatcroft's The Years of Hunger, was not read for this story.
