title: The Hunger
subtitle: The Soviet famine of 1930–33, from the steppe of Kazakhstan to Ukraine and the Volga, and the argument over what to call it
summary: Millions starved after Stalin's collectivisation; authors put the Union-wide toll between 5.5 and 14 million. Kazakhstan's famine is far less known, and Sarah Cameron puts the loss at more than a third of all Kazakhs. Parliaments, a Kyiv court and historians dispute whether it was planned and whether it was genocide; their competing accounts turn on what leaders knew, whom they targeted and what survivors could tell.
---

In the early 1930s the Soviet state took the grain and the herds from farms across the Union. Famine followed in Kazakhstan, Ukraine, the Volga and the North Caucasus. How many died depends on who counts: totals in the sources run from 5.5 million to about 14 million.

In Kazakhstan, herders slaughtered animals to eat and fled when the herds were gone. In Ukraine, officials took food from homes and stopped starving villagers leaving. Stalin received reports of suffering; his letters accused peasants of fighting Soviet power. Survivors, officials and the people who later counted the dead left different answers to how this happened, and why.
