title: The Lab
subtitle: Jesse Gelsinger, James Wilson and the university that held a stake
summary: From the 1980 Bayh-Dole Act to Jesse Gelsinger’s death in 1999, the FDA’s allegations, a 2005 settlement without admissions and James Wilson’s 2009 account. A closing look at the studies that counted financial ties and research outcomes.
---

James Wilson and the University of Pennsylvania both held shares in Genovo, the company with rights to license his laboratory’s discoveries. Jesse Gelsinger, eighteen, died four days after receiving an experimental gene therapy at Penn in September 1999. The FDA alleged safety and consent violations; in 2005 Wilson agreed to five years without sponsoring FDA-regulated trials and at least five without unrestricted human research, while he and Penn admitted none of the government’s allegations and Penn denied that financial gain played any part in the trial.

Who stood to gain, and who bore the risk? The road begins with a law intended to turn publicly funded discoveries into useful inventions. It follows one trial through the family’s questions, the agency’s allegations and Wilson’s answer, before weighing studies of the wider pattern.

  narration: "**Beyond one laboratory.** Wilson’s account leaves a wider question: how common were financial ties, and did they track the answers research produced? In a 2003 review, Justin Bekelman and colleagues screened 1,664 citations and included 37 studies; Bekelman extracted their data. They estimated that about a quarter of investigators had industry affiliations and roughly two thirds of academic institutions held equity in start-ups sponsoring research there. Eight articles covering 1,140 studies linked sponsorship to pro-industry conclusions, with pooled odds of 3.60 times those for other sponsorship. That association counted a pattern; it did not determine why Jesse’s trial continued."
