title: The Deported
subtitle: Whole peoples moved by order of the Soviet state, 1937 to 1944, and what the records say about who gave the orders, who died, who came home
summary: Between 1937 and 1944 the Soviet state moved whole peoples, from Koreans in the Far East to the Chechens, Ingush and Crimean Tatars, to Kazakhstan, Central Asia and Siberia. Here are the decrees, the NKVD's own counts and the scholars who dispute them, Khrushchev's partial admission in 1956, and who was and was not allowed home.
---

The order of 21 August 1937, dated at the Kremlin, bore Stalin’s and Molotov’s signatures; NKVD troops took families from their homes and put them on trains. From the Koreans of the Far East in 1937 to the Meskhetian Turks in 1944, the Soviet state deported whole peoples to Kazakhstan, Central Asia and Siberia. Officials accused them of espionage or betrayal, including children and soldiers fighting in the Red Army.

The journeys killed thousands; forced labor, hunger and disease killed many more in exile. Families shared food, saved letters and refused orders that would keep them from returning. Some won permission to go home in 1957. Others faced another generation of petitions, and recognition of the wrong did not restore the homes they had lost.
