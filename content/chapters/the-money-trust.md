title: The Money Trust
subtitle: How the men who built industrial America were connected, and what the record shows was done where their companies worked
summary: In 1912 Congress asked whether a few bankers held the country's money. Here are the men on the same boards, and what the record shows about Johnstown, Homestead, Pullman, Alabama and Ludlow, from testimony, reports and their own words.
---

In December 1912, J. P. Morgan told Congress that credit began with a banker’s judgment of a man’s character. Two months later, the Pujo committee counted 341 directorships in 112 corporations held by men from Morgan’s firm and four banks grouped with it. Its majority called the connections a money trust; the minority rejected the name but found dangerous concentration of credit.

Behind those board seats were decisions made at a dam, a steel works, a company town and mining camps. Frick hired guards, Carnegie sent a cable, miners and their families fled gunfire, and the men whose fortunes funded foundations came before investigators to explain themselves. Their letters and testimony put their reasons beside the consequences for people who worked and lived there.
