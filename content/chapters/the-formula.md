title: The Formula
subtitle: What the record shows about Nestlé, infant formula and babies in poor countries, from a Bern court to the WHO code
summary: Mothers sought feeding help, doctors changed hospital routines, and campaigners challenged how companies sold formula. Nestlé defended its milk. A Bern verdict, a boycott and a global marketing code followed.
---

In excerpts translated and published by the Bern campaigners, a Bern court found in 1976 that powdered milk prepared with dirty water, dirty bottles or too little powder can kill an infant, that Nestlé's advertising in poor countries had gone further than at home, and that Nestlé would have to change it to be spared the charge of immoral conduct. It also found that the quality of Nestlé's milk was not contested, and that in criminal law Nestlé could not be held responsible for the deaths, because third parties' actions broke the chain. WHO's 1981 code says formula is needed when mothers do not breastfeed, and asks that its marketing be restrained.

Studies in Brazil and five other countries found far higher risks of death from infection among babies not breastfed. The estimates measure different things: ten million preventable cases of illness a year in developing countries, partly a guess by paediatrician Derrick Jelliffe in 1978; a million preventable infant deaths a year, estimated by UNICEF's James Grant in 1981; and 823,000 preventable under-five deaths a year in a 2016 Lancet model. These estimates concern feeding across populations; they do not count one company's customers.

Mothers sought help, doctors changed nursery routines, executives defended their products and campaigners asked shoppers to stop buying them. The Bern verdict and the marketing code drew different lines around responsibility. Nestlé, Abbott, Bristol-Myers and American Home Products each had their own part in the dispute.
